How I got there

Six steps, in the order they happened. Each one opens with the single thing that mattered most.

01The problem 02Research 03Four decisions 04The system 05Policy in the UI 06Impact
01

The product worked. It didn't look like it.

The software was solid. The interface made people think it wasn't.

Cyberstar helps compliance leaders in federal defense and cybersecurity agencies track whether their people meet DoD 8140 qualification requirements: who's compliant, who's at risk, and what's due when. The logic underneath was solid. The surface wasn't keeping up.

The interface had grown feature by feature into a dated, navy, utilitarian shell with no shared visual language. Components were styled one-off, so the same idea looked different on every screen. For a product being evaluated by security-conscious government buyers, an interface that felt like it was from the 90s quietly undercut trust. It also created support tickets we didn't need, because when something looks unfinished, people assume it's broken.

02

I asked the people who buy it and the people who sell it

I didn't want this to come down to what I thought looked good.

Before I touched a color, I talked to customers, to prospects, and to our sales team. Sales hears the objections a designer never hears, and it turned out the reasons people hesitate had almost nothing to do with what I would have guessed. Two findings shaped everything that followed.

groupsCustomers · prospects · sales

In government and military contexts, purple reads as neutral. It doesn't carry the service-branch or party associations that blues, reds and greens do.

quizSupport & customer success

Users lean on Cyberstar to interpret vague 8140 policy. The questions they ask support are questions the interface could answer in place.

visibilityPerception signal

A dated look made a working product feel unreliable, which made buyers warier and support busier.

03

Four decisions, each one traceable

Every one of them came from something a customer or a salesperson actually said.

Every choice traces back to something a customer actually said, so I could defend it in a room instead of just liking the look of it.

palette

Purple as the brand

Purple (#534AB7) became the primary brand color and the color of every call to action. It was the one color research said reads as neutral and credible to this audience.

auto_awesome

Modernize on purpose

A cleaner, more current look leans on the aesthetic–usability effect: interfaces that look better are perceived as working better. The goal was fewer "is this broken?" tickets and a clearer sense of value.

menu_book

Embed the policy

Tooltips, descriptions, and status components carry 8140 policy knowledge into the interface at the moment someone needs it. The thing customers pay us for is now part of the product, not just our support inbox.

deployed_code

Tokenize with engineering

Working with our lead engineer, the system is built on design tokens and reusable components, so changing the brand is a one-line change and new screens come out consistent without anyone policing them.

Status components, tokenized

tuneOne component, five states. Each has its own color ramp, tooltip copy, and selected or unselected treatment.

◴ Qual in progressFamily: Attention · amber

Before the system, each of these was styled one-off wherever it appeared, so the same idea looked different on the dashboard, the profile, and the compliance review. Tokenizing them meant the policy meaning and the visual meaning stay locked together, and a new status gets added once instead of five times.

The sidebar, the active nav pill, the logo mark and the avatar are all steps on a single 245° ramp, the brand purple's own hue. That is what makes the chrome and the accent read as one family rather than two colors that happen to sit near each other, and it keeps brand purple clear of the purple that carries meaning in the status system.

Naming matters as much as the values. Each step is a primitive (purple.700) with a semantic alias pointing at it (chrome.surface, action.primary). Components reference the alias, never the primitive, so the next rebrand is a change of values instead of another restyle.

Running this on the whole set caught one real failure. The amber that marks a running qualification clock started at #A66900, which lands at 4.16:1 on its own background. That is close enough to pass a glance and fail an audit. Darkening it to #8F5C00 puts it at 5.2:1 without changing how the status reads.

Color is also never the only signal. Each of the five statuses carries a glyph and a word, so the meaning survives a grayscale print, a projector, and a person who does not see the difference between the amber and the green.

One component, documented

descriptionThe status pill is the component most likely to get misused, so it is the one with rules written down.

◎ Active waiver
Containerradius.full, 1px border, background and border both from the status family
GlyphFixed per family. Carries the meaning when color cannot.
LabelPolicy wording, never a paraphrase. Sentence case, no truncation.
FamilyOne of five: met, unmet, attention, policy, neutral.

checkUse it when

  • Showing where a person or requirement stands right now against 8140.
  • The state is one of the five. If it is not, the taxonomy needs to change, not the component.
  • The label can be the policy term in full.

closeDo not use it for

  • Anything a person clicks. A pill that looks tappable but is not is the fastest way to lose trust in the table.
  • Requirement types like Foundational or Residential-OJT. Those have their own reserved hues so a type is never read as a status.
  • Counts or filters. Those are chips, and they get the neutral treatment.
  • A waiver shown in green or red. A waiver is neither passing nor failing, and coloring it as either misreports compliance to a government customer.
05

Policy, embedded where the decision gets made

The thing customers pay us for was living in our support inbox. Now it lives in the product.

DoD 8140 is simultaneously vague and unforgiving. Agencies buy Cyberstar to interpret it for them, so the interpretation belongs in the interface, not in a support ticket.

This is the part of the rebrand that isn't really visual. Our customers rely on us to tell them what the directive actually requires: which training counts as foundational, how long the clock runs, whether a waiver means someone is covered. When that knowledge lives only in our team's heads, every ambiguity becomes an email. When it lives in the interface, the person hits it at the exact moment they need it and moves on.

So every term that carries policy weight is a hover target with the rule written out and, where there is one, the citation. Not a help center someone has to go find. Not a tour on first login. The definition sits on the word, at the moment the decision is being made.

Why it matters commercially. Three things follow from this, and none of them are about how the product looks. Support volume should fall, because the most common questions are answered before they get asked. The product stops being a database that stores compliance records and starts being the thing that explains compliance, which is much harder for a competitor to copy. And in a demo, a government buyer hovering a term and seeing the directive cited back to them is the moment the software reads as authoritative.

That last point is the connection between this section and the rebrand. A polished interface makes a product look credible. Embedded policy makes it be credible. The visual work buys attention; this is what the attention is for.

06

Anticipated impact

It's only partly rolled out, so these are targets with a way to measure each one, not results.

The system is partially rolled out, so these are the outcomes it was designed to produce and the way each one gets measured. They are not results I can claim yet.

Stating it this way is deliberate. A rebrand that reports numbers before it has shipped is telling you something about the designer, not the design.

Fewer support tickets about interpretation

The most common questions we field are policy questions, not software questions: what counts as foundational, how long the clock runs, whether a waiver covers someone. Those three now answer themselves on hover.

MEASURE · TICKET VOLUME BY CATEGORY AGAINST THE PRE-ROLLOUT BASELINE
Less one-off styling as the product grows

Every component now resolves to a token rather than a hard-coded value, so a new screen inherits the system instead of reinventing it. This is the outcome most within our control.

MEASURE · SHARE OF NEW UI BUILT FROM LIBRARY COMPONENTS
Stronger credibility in demos

Government buyers evaluate on trust as much as function. An interface that cites the directive back to them argues for our expertise before a salesperson says anything.

MEASURE · QUALITATIVE, FROM SALES AND CUSTOMER SUCCESS AFTER DEMOS

The first of these is the one I'd hold myself to. If support volume on policy questions doesn't move, the embedded-policy strategy didn't work, however good the interface looks.
