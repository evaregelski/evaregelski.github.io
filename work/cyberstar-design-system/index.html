Cyberstar

Making compliance software look as trustworthy as it is

A ground-up rebrand and design system for the platform federal defense agencies use to manage DoD 8140 workforce compliance — from a dated navy interface to a tokenized purple system that embeds policy knowledge where decisions get made.

01

The product worked. It didn't look like it.

Cyberstar helps compliance leaders in federal defense and cybersecurity agencies track whether their people meet DoD 8140 qualification requirements — who's compliant, who's at risk, and what's due when. The logic underneath was solid. The surface wasn't keeping up.

The interface had grown feature by feature into a dated, navy, utilitarian shell with no shared visual language. Components were styled one-off, so the same idea looked different on every screen. For a product being evaluated by security-conscious government buyers, an interface that felt like it was from the 90s quietly undercut trust — and it generated avoidable support tickets, because when something looks unfinished, people assume it's broken.

02

Research: I let customers, prospects, and sales set the direction

A rebrand can't run on taste. Before touching color, I talked with existing customers and prospects and worked directly with sales, whose conversations surface objections a designer never hears firsthand. Two findings shaped everything that followed.

groupsCustomers · prospects · sales

In government and military contexts, purple reads as neutral — it doesn't carry service-branch or partisan associations the way blues, reds, and greens can.

quizSupport & customer success

Users lean on Cyberstar to interpret vague 8140 policy. The questions they ask support are questions the interface could answer in place.

visibilityPerception signal

A dated look made a working product feel unreliable — inflating perceived risk for buyers and support load for users.

03

Four decisions the research pointed to

Every choice traces back to a finding — so the system is defensible to stakeholders, not just pretty.

palette

Purple as the brand

Purple (#534AB7) became the primary brand color and the color of every call to action — the one hue research said reads as neutral and credible to this audience.

auto_awesome

Modernize on purpose

A cleaner, more current look leans on the aesthetic–usability effect: interfaces that look better are perceived as working better. The goal was fewer "is this broken?" tickets and a clearer sense of value.

menu_book

Embed the policy

Tooltips, descriptions, and status components carry 8140 policy knowledge into the UI at the point of decision — turning Cyberstar's role as policy interpreter into a designed feature.

deployed_code

Tokenize with engineering

Working with our lead engineer, the system is built on design tokens and reusable components — so the brand is a one-line repoint, and new features ship consistent by default.

Before the system, each of these was styled one-off wherever it appeared, so the same idea looked different on the dashboard, the profile, and the compliance review. Tokenizing them meant the policy meaning and the visual meaning stay locked together — and a new status can be added once rather than five times.

The sidebar, the active nav pill, the logo mark and the avatar are all steps on a single 245° ramp — the brand purple's own hue. That is what makes the chrome and the accent read as one family rather than two colors that happen to sit near each other, and it keeps brand purple clear of the purple that carries meaning in the status system.

Naming matters as much as the values. Each step is a primitive (purple.700) with a semantic alias pointing at it (chrome.surface, action.primary). Components reference the alias, never the primitive — so the next rebrand is a change of values rather than another restyling.

05

Policy, embedded where the decision gets made

DoD 8140 is simultaneously vague and unforgiving. Agencies buy Cyberstar to interpret it for them — so the interpretation belongs in the interface, not in a support ticket.

This is the part of the rebrand that isn't really visual. Our customers rely on us to tell them what the directive actually requires: which training counts as foundational, how long the clock runs, whether a waiver means someone is covered. When that knowledge lives only in our team's heads, every ambiguity becomes an email. When it lives in the interface, the person hits it at the exact moment they need it and moves on.

So every term that carries policy weight is a hover target with the rule written out and, where there is one, the citation. Not a help center someone has to go find. Not a tour on first login. The definition sits on the word, at the moment the decision is being made.

Why it matters commercially. Three things follow from this, and none of them are about how the product looks. Support volume should fall, because the most common questions are answered before they get asked. The product stops being a database that stores compliance records and starts being the thing that explains compliance — which is a much harder position for a competitor to copy. And in a demo, a government buyer hovering a term and seeing the directive cited back to them is the moment the software reads as authoritative.

That last point is the connection between this section and the rebrand. A polished interface makes a product look credible. Embedded policy makes it be credible. The visual work buys attention; this is what the attention is for.

06

Anticipated impact

The system is partially rolled out, so these are the outcomes it was designed to produce and the way each one gets measured — not results I can claim yet.

Stating it this way is deliberate. A rebrand that reports numbers before it has shipped is telling you something about the designer, not the design.

Fewer support tickets about interpretation

The most common questions we field are policy questions, not software questions: what counts as foundational, how long the clock runs, whether a waiver covers someone. Those three now answer themselves on hover.

MEASURE — TICKET VOLUME BY CATEGORY AGAINST THE PRE-ROLLOUT BASELINE
Less one-off styling as the product grows

Every component now resolves to a token rather than a hard-coded value, so a new screen inherits the system instead of reinventing it. This is the outcome most within our control.

MEASURE — SHARE OF NEW UI BUILT FROM LIBRARY COMPONENTS
Stronger credibility in demos

Government buyers evaluate on trust as much as function. An interface that cites the directive back to them argues for our expertise before a salesperson says anything.

MEASURE — QUALITATIVE, FROM SALES AND CUSTOMER SUCCESS AFTER DEMOS

The first of these is the one I'd hold myself to. If support volume on policy questions doesn't move, the embedded-policy strategy didn't work, however good the interface looks.
